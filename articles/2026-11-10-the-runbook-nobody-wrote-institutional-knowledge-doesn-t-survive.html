The Runbook Nobody Wrote: Institutional Knowledge Doesn't Survive in People

openedterminal.com  |  November 10, 2026  |  Tech / News

Every engineering organization has at least one person whose departure would be described internally as "unsurvivable." Not in those exact words, which nobody would put in writing, but in calendar terms: two engineers are already blocked on them, the on-call rotation quietly routes around their blind spots, and a specific class of incident, the weird one, the one that has happened three times and never once resolved at root cause, goes to them first because they are the only person who has seen it. This person is often called a hero, which is accurate in the mythological sense but technically backwards. They are not the strength of the organization. They are its single point of failure, wearing the costume of one.

The standard diagnosis is that they document too little, and the standard fix is documentation, usually mandated after a resignation scare: a sprint for writing runbooks, a wiki reorganization, a Slack message announcing that going forward, everyone must write things down. It fails every time, for reasons worth understanding, because the knowledge that matters is not actually in this person's head in the form of sentences. It is in the form of pattern recognition accumulated across incidents, the instinct that says this alert looks like the 2019 thing and therefore check the disk before the network. You cannot type pattern recognition into a wiki. You can only expose other people to the pattern under supervision, which is a completely different engineering problem than the one documentation sprints pretend to solve.

This is why the runbook is the wrong unit of measure. A runbook is optimized for the incident that recurs identically, and incidents almost never recur identically, they recur structurally. The disk fills, the cache expires, the certificate rotates, the retry storm hits, and the structure is always some variant of a queue backing up behind a finite resource, while the surface symptoms are always different enough to defeat keyword search. What the hero actually provides is mapping: the ability to translate a new symptom into a known structure. The correct artifact is therefore not a document but a trained person who shares the map, and the correct process is not a documentation sprint but a rotation of exposure, where the weird pages get routed to two people instead of one, deliberately, even when it is slower and costs more.

Organizations resist this because the arithmetic looks bad in the short term. Pairing on the weird incident is slower than having the hero fix it. Shadowing is unbillable. The hero himself often resists, though rarely for status reasons and usually because the first ten translation attempts made by others will be bad, and correcting bad translations in real time during an incident is genuinely unpleasant work. But the long-run arithmetic is brutal in the other direction. The organization that trains two people per pattern class recovers from the departure of either. The one that trains nobody re-negotiates its resilience with every resignation, and eventually loses that negotiation at the worst possible moment, which is what "unsurvivable" turns out to mean in practice.

The practical program is smaller than people expect. Route the weird pages to two responders by default, rotating, with the hero explicitly second, not first, and say out loud that this is deliberate and will be slower for a quarter. Require that every incident review produce not a runbook but a one-paragraph structural summary: what class of failure this was, what the map looks like, what the checks are in order. Write those paragraphs into a shared index, not a wiki graveyard, and have the second responder, not the hero, give the five-minute walkthrough at the next team meeting. The point is never the document. The point is that a second mind has now walked the same structure once, under supervision, and minds reproduce themselves only by walking.

Institutional knowledge does not live in people. It lives in the distance between people, and distance is a thing you can pay down quarterly, in small installments, or default on all at once during a resignations. Choose the installment plan.
