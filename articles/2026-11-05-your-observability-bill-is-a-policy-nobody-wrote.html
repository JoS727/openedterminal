Your Observability Bill Is a Policy Nobody Wrote

openedterminal.com  |  November 5, 2026  |  Tech / News

Look at the observability line on your cloud invoice and ask one question: if a committee met in a room and had to approve this number, with line items attached, would every item survive? In most orgs of meaningful size, the answer is no — and the reason is that no committee ever met. Observability spend is almost never decided; it is accumulated. Every team turned on a default verbosity, every service shipped with a metrics exporter someone installed once, every debugging session ended with the trace level left at debug because turning it down was unglamorous and someone might need it "next time." Twelve months later the bill is a seven-figure policy, and the person who could actually change it is the same person who was busy at the time.

The deeper cause is that logs and metrics have a pricing model that lies to the engineer. Writing a log line at debug level is free at the keyboard. The cost arrives later, amortized across every gigabyte ingested, every index maintained, every retention day, every query that has to scan a corpus nobody curated. The engineer who writes 40,000 log lines per deploy never sees the invoice; the platform team that sees the invoice did not write the lines. This is the classic externality shape, and companies respond to it the way they respond to all externalities: they install a chargeback mechanism, which changes who is angry without changing the behavior.

The honest fix starts with retention, because retention is the highest-leverage decision nobody has ever explicitly made. Ask your observability vendor how much of the bill is storage of data that will never be queried again — the answer is routinely between 60 and 90 percent. Most of what teams call "debugging data" is never debugged with. The workable policy tiers it: full-fidelity debug logs live for days, not months; aggregate metrics live for a year; raw everything goes to cheap object storage with a document explaining how hard it is to get data back out. Then let someone actually test the restore path, because unexercised cold storage is not a safety net, it is a monthly bill that buys the feeling of one.

The second fix is semantic, and it matters more than any vendor negotiation: log lines should carry intent, and volume should be a reviewed artifact, like code. A pull request that adds 3,000 new log statements at info level should be as visible as one that adds 3,000 lines of logic. It almost never is, because logs live outside the code review economy. Attach the consequence to the change — put projected ingestion volume in the PR description the same way you would put bundle size — and the behavior corrects itself within a quarter. Engineers do not write voluminous logs out of malice; they write them because nothing ever presented the cost next to the diff.

Be suspicious of the counterargument you will hear, because it is usually a real concern wearing inflation as armor. "We need it when something breaks" is legitimate; debug verbosity on a flaky service is genuinely cheap insurance. But the honest version of that argument is scoped: an individual service, turned up during an investigation, turned back down as part of the same runbook. The dishonest version is fleet-wide, permanent, and defended as though turning data off were riskier than a recurring seven-figure spend with an unbounded growth curve. One of these is engineering judgment. The other is accretion with a budget line.

What to do this month, concretely: pull the top ten sources of ingest by volume, not by importance, and read them for ten minutes — most orgs find either a retry loop logging whole response bodies or a single path emitting the same successful heartbeat at a rate that would embarrass a nuclear plant. Set a retention policy in writing with an owner who is not the vendor. Kill no pipeline before it has a replacement query path. Then delete one thing, measure nothing lost for a quarter, and delete something else. A company that cannot say why it pays for its telemetry does not have observability; it has a subscription to its own anxiety, auto-renewed.
