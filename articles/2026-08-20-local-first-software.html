The Rise of Local-First Software: Why Your Data Belongs on Your Machine

August 20, 2026  |  Opened Terminal  |  Tech

For over a decade, the prevailing wisdom in software design has been simple: put everything in the cloud. Your documents, your photos, your notes, your passwords, your code repositories, your project management tools. The promise was convenience. You could access your data from any device, share it with anyone, and never worry about backups because the servers handled that for you. It sounded like a fair deal, and for a while, it was.

But somewhere along the way, the trade-offs started to feel heavier. You noticed it when your note-taking app introduced a pricing tier that locked your own notes behind a paywall. You noticed it when a service you depended on shut down overnight and took your data with it. You noticed it when your internet went down and suddenly you could not open a single file. You noticed it when you read the umpteenth breach notification email telling you that your personal information was exposed in a database you never opted into directly.

That growing frustration has a name now. It is called local-first software, and it is one of the most important shifts in application design happening right now.

What Is Local-First Software?

Local-first software is a design philosophy where the primary copy of your data lives on your device, not on a server you do not control. The application runs locally, reads and writes to a local database, and works fully without an internet connection. Networking becomes an optional enhancement rather than a hard requirement.

This is not the same as "offline mode" bolted onto a cloud app. In a true local-first architecture, the local database is the source of truth. Synchronization to other devices or to a relay server happens in the background, when it can, without blocking your work. If the sync server disappears tomorrow, your data is still right there on your machine, intact and fully accessible.

The term was popularized by a 2019 paper titled "Local-First Software" by the team at Ink & Switch, the research lab behind CRDT-based document editing. Their argument was straightforward: the cloud computing revolution gave us great collaboration tools, but it also stripped users of ownership over their data. Local-first was their attempt to get the best of both worlds: real-time collaboration and offline capability, plus user ownership.

The Core Principles

Local-first software is built on a set of principles that distinguish it from traditional cloud apps:

1. No Spinners

When you open a local-first app, your data is already there. There is no loading state while the app fetches from a remote API. The local database responds in milliseconds. Every interaction feels instant because it is.

2. Offline by Default

The app works on a plane, on a train, in a tunnel, or during an outage. You can read, write, search, and edit without asking a server for permission. Syncing happens when connectivity returns, and it happens transparently through conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs) or similar mechanisms.

3. Your Data Is Yours

The primary copy lives on your device in a format you can inspect, export, and back up yourself. You are not renting access to your own information. If the company that makes the app goes out of business, your data survives.

4. Multi-Device Sync as a Feature, Not a Dependency

Syncing across devices is still valuable, and local-first apps offer it. But it is layered on top of the local-first foundation, not underneath it. The sync relay can be a managed service, a self-hosted server, or even peer-to-peer over your local network.

5. Collaboration Without Centralization

Multiple people can edit the same document simultaneously through CRDTs that merge changes deterministically without conflicts. No server has to arbitrate. No one has to lock a file and tell the others to wait.

The Technology Behind It

Local-first software would not be practical without a specific set of technologies that matured over the last several years. The most important is the CRDT, or Conflict-free Replicated Data Type.

A CRDT is a data structure that can be edited independently on multiple devices and then merged without conflicts. Because every edit is associated with a logical clock or a unique identifier, the merge operation is deterministic: every device that receives the same set of edits will converge to the same state, regardless of the order in which edits arrive. This is what makes real-time collaboration possible without a central authority resolving conflicts.

Libraries like Automerge, Yjs, and Y-Sweet have made CRDTs accessible to web developers. Automerge, created by the Ink & Switch team, is a JSON-like CRDT that integrates cleanly with JavaScript applications. Yjs is a widely adopted CRDT implementation that powers collaborative editing in projects ranging from text editors to whiteboard apps.

On the storage side, SQLite has become the workhorse of local-first applications. It is embedded, fast, reliable, and ships on every major operating system. Tools like PowerSync and ElectricSQL add sync layers on top of SQLite, giving developers a local database that can synchronize with a remote Postgres instance when connectivity allows.

Real-World Local-First Apps

The theory is compelling, but the proof is in the products. A growing number of applications are built on local-first principles, and they span categories from note-taking to development tools.

Obsidian

Obsidian stores all your notes as plain Markdown files in a local folder. There is no proprietary database, no vendor lock-in. You can open the same folder in any text editor. Obsidian Sync and Obsidian Publish are optional paid services layered on top, but the core experience is entirely local. This design has won Obsidian a fiercely loyal user base of developers, researchers, and writers who value data ownership.

Linear

Linear, the project management tool, uses a local-first sync engine that caches all your project data in the browser. Actions feel instant because they are applied locally first and then synced to the server. The result is an app that feels dramatically faster than competing tools that wait for server round-trips on every interaction.

Notable Mentions

Other apps embracing local-first or hybrid approaches include Logseq, Reflect, Anytype, Triplit, and the evolving ecosystem around the Local-First Web conference. The movement is gaining real commercial traction, not just academic interest.

Why Developers Should Care

If you build software, local-first architecture changes your calculus in several ways:

Performance becomes a feature. When every read is local, you stop building loading skeletons and spinners. Your app feels fast because it is fast. Users notice this immediately. The perceived performance of a local-first app is often the thing that converts users from a competitor.

Your backend gets simpler. You do not need a high-availability API that handles every read and write in real time. You need a sync relay, which is a narrower and more manageable problem. Many local-first apps use a generic sync server, reducing custom backend code significantly.

You reduce your attack surface. When the primary data store is on the user's device, you are not sitting on a honeypot of everyone's data. A breach of your sync relay does not expose the full content of every user's documents. This is a meaningful security and liability improvement.

You avoid the hostage problem. Users who know they can export their data at any time are more willing to trust you with it. This is the same logic that made RSS and open formats powerful. Data portability is not just ethical, it is a competitive advantage.

Challenges and Trade-Offs

Local-first is not a silver bullet. There are genuine challenges that teams should weigh before committing:

Sync complexity. CRDTs handle conflict resolution automatically, but they introduce their own complexity. Understanding merge semantics, debugging sync issues, and handling large document histories requires expertise. The tooling has improved, but it is still less mature than traditional CRUD-over-REST patterns.

Storage limits. Local databases have limits. If your app deals with gigabytes of data per user, storing everything locally on every device is impractical. Some apps use a hybrid model where only the most recent or most relevant data is cached locally, and older data is fetched on demand.

Multi-user access control. When data lives on a server, it is straightforward to enforce permissions and sharing rules. When data is replicated across devices, access control becomes more complex. You need to think about encryption, key management, and revocation in a distributed context.

The collaboration paradox. CRDTs resolve conflicts deterministically, but deterministic does not always mean sensible. Two people editing the same paragraph can produce a merged result that is technically conflict-free but semantically wrong. Designing UX that handles these edge cases gracefully is still an evolving art.

The Economic Argument

Beyond the technical merits, there is a compelling economic case for local-first architecture. Cloud infrastructure costs scale with your user base. Every user who opens your app triggers API calls, database queries, and bandwidth usage that you pay for. In a local-first model, most of those operations happen on the user's device, which they already paid for. Your server costs drop to the sync relay and whatever optional services you provide.

For indie developers and small teams, this can be the difference between a sustainable business and a money-losing one. The ability to serve thousands of users with a modest sync server rather than a fleet of API instances changes the unit economics significantly.

Getting Started

If you want to experiment with local-first architecture, here is a practical starting point:

1. Pick a CRDT library. Automerge and Yjs are the two most popular options for JavaScript applications. Both have good documentation and active communities.

2. Choose a local storage layer. SQLite via WA-SQLite or absurd-sql works in the browser. For native apps, SQLite is already available on every platform.

3. Add a sync layer. You can use a managed service like Y-Sweet or PowerSync, or self-host a sync relay using libraries like y-websocket or Automerge's networking helpers.

4. Design your app around the local database first. Every read comes from local storage. Every write goes to local storage first and is then queued for sync. Build your UI to be optimistic and assume success.

5. Test offline behavior rigorously. The whole point is that the app works without connectivity. Use your browser's offline throttling and airplane mode on mobile devices to verify that nothing breaks when the network disappears.

The Bigger Picture

Local-first software is part of a broader movement toward user sovereignty over data. It sits alongside concerns about privacy, surveillance capitalism, and platform lock-in. When your data lives on your machine in formats you control, the balance of power shifts. You are not a tenant renting access to your own thoughts. You are an owner.

The cloud is not going away. It remains the best tool for many jobs: large-scale computation, shared datasets, real-time communication at scale. But the default of putting everything in the cloud by reflex is being questioned by serious engineers who have seen the costs. Local-first is not about rejecting the cloud. It is about choosing when to use it and when to keep things close.

For a generation of developers who grew up building CRUD apps over REST APIs, local-first represents a paradigm shift. It requires thinking about data ownership, conflict resolution, and sync architecture in ways that traditional web development never demanded. The learning curve is real, but the payoff is applications that are faster, more private, more resilient, and more respectful of the people who use them.

That is a future worth building toward.
