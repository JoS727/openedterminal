Pod Disruption Budgets: The Unsung Hero of Kubernetes Production Stability

If you have run a Kubernetes cluster in production for any meaningful amount of time, you have likely experienced the quiet dread of a voluntary disruption: a node upgrade rolls through, a cluster autoscaler drains a node, and suddenly a critical service loses enough replicas to degrade its throughput. The mechanism that exists to prevent exactly this scenario is the Pod Disruption Budget, or PDB. It is one of those Kubernetes features that most operators know about in theory but consistently underuse or misconfigure in practice. This article walks through how PDBs work, what they protect against, the common pitfalls that defeat them, and a set of best practices for making them genuinely effective in production.

Voluntary vs. Involuntary Disruptions

Before diving into PDB mechanics, it is worth separating the two classes of disruption Kubernetes recognizes. Involuntary disruptions are things like hardware failures, kernel panics, node network partitions, or eviction due to resource pressure. Kubernetes cannot prevent these by policy. The system reacts to them after the fact through controllers like the ReplicaSet controller, which provisions replacement pods on healthy nodes.

Voluntary disruptions are initiated by humans or automated controllers. They include node drains for upgrades, cluster autoscaler scale-downs, pod deletions via kubectl, and Helm or ArgoCD rollouts. These are the disruptions that PDBs are designed to control. A PDB tells the Kubernetes scheduler and eviction API: "You may remove pods from this workload, but only down to a minimum threshold of availability." If removing a pod would breach that threshold, the eviction request is denied.

That distinction matters because teams frequently conflate the two. A PDB will not save you from a node going offline unexpectedly. It will save you from a kubectl drain or an autoscaler decision that would take a service below its minimum replica count. If your service needs protection from involuntary disruptions, the answer is redundancy across failure domains, anti-affinity rules, and multiple replicas, not a PDB.

How a PDB Actually Works

A Pod Disruption Budget is a simple Kubernetes resource. You define it against a workload using a label selector, and you specify either a minAvailable or a maxUnavailable constraint. Here is a straightforward example:

apiVersion: policy/v1
kind: PodDisruptionBudget
metadata:
  name: api-server-pdb
spec:
  minAvailable: 2
  selector:
    matchLabels:
      app: api-server

This PDB says that for any pod matching the label app: api-server, at least 2 pods must remain available at all times during voluntary disruptions. If the deployment currently has 4 replicas and a drain operation tries to evict the third pod (which would leave only 1 available), the eviction API returns a 429 Too Many Requests and the drain blocks.

You can also express the constraint as a percentage. minAvailable: 50% on a 6-replica deployment means at least 3 must stay up. Alternatively, maxUnavailable: 1 means at most 1 pod can be unavailable at any given time during a voluntary disruption. Both approaches are valid; the choice depends on whether your mental model is floor-based or ceiling-based.

The Eviction API Is the Enforcement Mechanism

One of the most misunderstood aspects of PDBs is how enforcement actually happens. A PDB is not enforced by the deployment controller or the ReplicaSet controller. It is enforced by the eviction API, which is invoked when you run kubectl drain or when the cluster autoscaler decides to remove a node. The eviction API checks all PDBs whose selectors match the pod being evicted and rejects the request if the result would violate any applicable budget.

This means that a direct kubectl delete pod does not respect PDBs. Deleting a pod directly is not an eviction. It is a deletion, and the API server allows it. If you need PDB protection, you must go through the eviction endpoint, either via kubectl drain (which uses evictions internally) or by creating an Eviction subresource explicitly.

Key Insight

PDBs protect against voluntary disruptions that route through the eviction API. They do not protect against direct pod deletion, node failures, or any involuntary disruption. Understanding this boundary is essential to using them correctly.

Common Misconfigurations

1. PDBs on Single-Replica Workloads

The most classic misconfiguration is applying a PDB with minAvailable: 1 to a Deployment that only runs 1 replica. This creates a situation where no voluntary eviction can ever succeed, because evicting the single pod would bring availability to 0. The drain hangs indefinitely. The cluster autoscaler cannot scale down the node. Node upgrades stall. This is one of the most common causes of "stuck drains" in production clusters.

If a workload genuinely requires a single replica, a PDB is not the right tool. You need to scale it to at least 2 replicas before a PDB with minAvailable: 1 makes sense. A PDB on a single replica is a loaded gun pointed at your upgrade pipeline.

2. Label Selector Mismatches

A PDB's selector must match the pods of the workload it intends to protect. If the selector does not match any pods, the PDB is effectively a no-op. Worse, if it accidentally matches pods across multiple workloads, you get cross-workload coupling where evicting a pod from service A can be blocked by a PDB intended for service B. Always verify your PDB selectors with kubectl get pdb and check the Expected Pods and Allowed Disruptions columns.

3. Conflicting PDBs

If multiple PDBs match the same set of pods, every one of them must allow the eviction. Two PDBs each requiring minAvailable: 3 on the same 4-replica deployment effectively means 3 must stay available, but if one says minAvailable: 3 and the other says minAvailable: 2, the stricter one wins. This is usually a mistake, not a design choice. Keep one PDB per workload unless you have a specific reason for multiple.

PDBs and the Cluster Autoscaler

The Kubernetes Cluster Autoscaler interacts closely with PDBs. When the autoscaler evaluates whether to remove a node, it simulates the drain process, including PDB checks. If a node's pods are protected by a PDB and draining would violate it, the autoscaler will not remove that node. This is desirable behavior in most cases, but it has a side effect: PDBs that are too aggressive can prevent the autoscaler from ever scaling down, leaving you with idle nodes that cost money.

The autoscaler has a configurable grace period (default 10 minutes via --max-graceful-termination-sec and related flags) during which it waits for PDB-protected pods to be drainable. If the PDB never relaxes, the node stays. In clusters with aggressive cost-optimization goals, this is a frequent source of waste. The solution is not to remove PDBs, but to right-size them: use percentage-based budgets or maxUnavailable on horizontally scalable workloads, and reserve strict minAvailable PDBs for stateful or quorum-based services.

PDBs for StatefulSets and Quorum Systems

Stateful workloads like etcd, ZooKeeper, Kafka with Kraft, or Cassandra have quorum requirements. Losing more than a certain number of replicas simultaneously can cause data loss or split-brain. For these, PDBs are not just a convenience; they are a data safety mechanism. An etcd cluster of 3 nodes can tolerate 1 failure. A PDB with maxUnavailable: 1 ensures that a node drain never takes 2 etcd members offline at once.

For a 5-node etcd cluster, the quorum is 3, so you can tolerate 2 failures. A PDB with maxUnavailable: 1 is conservative but safe. Some operators use maxUnavailable: 1 even when 2 would be safe, because drain operations during upgrades are sequential and the conservative budget provides a margin against unexpected pod startup delays on replacement nodes.

The key principle for stateful workloads is that the PDB should reflect the quorum math of the underlying system, not a generic "keep most pods up" heuristic. A generic PDB on a quorum system is worse than no PDB if it gives a false sense of security while still allowing quorum loss.

Best Practices Checklist

  • Right-size the budget. Use maxUnavailable for horizontally scalable stateless workloads. Use minAvailable for quorum-based or stateful systems where a floor is more natural.
  • Never put a PDB on a single-replica deployment. Scale the deployment first, then add the PDB.
  • Verify selectors match. Check kubectl get pdb output regularly. A PDB matching zero pods is dead weight.
  • One PDB per workload. Avoid multiple overlapping PDBs unless you have a specific, documented reason.
  • Use percentage-based budgets for elastic workloads. If your replica count changes frequently via HPA, minAvailable: 50% scales with you. A hard minAvailable: 5 breaks if you scale below 10.
  • Account for pod startup time. A PDB that allows 1 disruption is fine if replacement pods start in seconds. If startup takes 60 seconds, consider a stricter budget or pre-scaling during maintenance windows.
  • Test your PDBs during maintenance. Do a planned drain in a staging cluster and observe whether evictions succeed or hang. A PDB that has never been tested is a PDB you cannot trust.

Integrating PDBs into GitOps

If you manage clusters with ArgoCD or Flux, PDBs should live in the same Git repository as the workloads they protect. This ensures they are applied alongside deployments and version-controlled. A common pattern is to ship a PDB as part of a Helm chart's templates/ directory, conditionally enabled based on replica count:

{{- if .Values.podDisruptionBudget.enabled }}
apiVersion: policy/v1
kind: PodDisruptionBudget
metadata:
  name: {{ .Release.Name }}-pdb
spec
  minAvailable: {{ .Values.podDisruptionBudget.minAvailable }}
  selector:
    matchLabels:
      app: {{ .Release.Name }}
{{- end }}

This approach lets platform teams ship sensible defaults while allowing application teams to override the budget for workloads with specific availability requirements. It also means the PDB is visible in code review, not a hidden kubectl apply someone ran manually and forgot to document.

The Deeper Lesson: Explicit Failure Domains

PDBs are a microcosm of a broader principle in distributed systems engineering: you must make your failure domains explicit. Kubernetes gives you tools to express availability requirements, but those tools only work if you use them deliberately and test them. A PDB is a contract between your workload and the cluster's disruption controllers. If the contract is vague, contradictory, or untested, the cluster will find the gap at the worst possible moment.

For a deeper grounding in the principles of distributed system reliability and the tradeoffs involved in designing for availability, Designing Data-Intensive Applications by Martin Kleppmann remains the most thorough treatment of replication, quorum, and consistency models that underpin decisions like PDB configuration. And for teams getting serious about Kubernetes operations end to end, Kubernetes Up and Running by Brendan Burns, Joe Beda, and Kelsey Hightower covers the practical layer of cluster management that pairs naturally with PDB strategy.

The teams that sleep well at night are the ones that have thought through these failure modes before they happen, codified the requirements as Kubernetes resources, and validated them through testing. PDBs are a small resource with an outsized impact. Treat them accordingly.
