The Raspberry Pi 400: A Full Computer in a Keyboard for $70

If you told someone in 1995 that you could buy a complete computer for seventy dollars, they would have laughed you out of the room. A budget PC back then ran you over a thousand dollars, came in a beige tower, and could barely run a web browser without catching fire. Fast forward to today and the landscape could not be more different. The Raspberry Pi 400 Personal Computer Kit ships an entire ARM-based desktop computer inside a keyboard chassis for the price of a nice dinner out. It is not a toy. It is not a novelty. It is a genuinely capable machine that can handle real work, and it represents something the tech industry has been quietly moving toward for years: making computing accessible again.

What Is It, Actually?

The Raspberry Pi 400 is not a peripheral. It is not a keyboard with a Raspberry Pi attached to it. It is a Raspberry Pi 4 with 4GB of RAM integrated directly into a compact keyboard housing. You open the box, plug it into a TV or monitor with a micro HDMI cable, connect a mouse, insert the pre-installed microSD card, and you are looking at a desktop. That is the entire setup process. There is no tower. There is no case fan. There is no BIOS to configure. You plug it in and it works.

Under the hood, you get a quad-core Broadcom BCM2711 processor running at 1.8 GHz, 4GB of LPDDR4 RAM, dual-band Wi-Fi, Bluetooth 5.0, gigabit Ethernet, two USB 3.0 ports, one USB 2.0 port, a microSD card slot for storage, and two micro HDMI ports capable of driving two 4K displays simultaneously. The GPIO header sits along the back edge, same as any other Raspberry Pi, so you can still wire up sensors, motors, LED strips, and whatever else you dream up. It runs Raspberry Pi OS, which is a Debian-based Linux distribution that comes with a browser, office suite, programming tools, and educational software pre-installed.

The Keyboard Form Factor Matters More Than You Think

The decision to put the board inside a keyboard is not just about aesthetics. It solves a real problem that has plagued single-board computers since the beginning: cables. A standard Raspberry Pi setup requires a case, a power supply, a USB hub for peripherals, an HDMI cable, and somewhere to put the board itself. That is five things before you have a working computer. The Pi 400 reduces that to two: power and display. The keyboard IS the case. The keyboard IS the board. The ports are right there on the back.

This matters because it removes friction. If you are a teacher setting up a classroom of twenty units, you do not want to manage twenty tangles of cables and twenty separate cases. You want to hand a student a keyboard and a mouse and say "plug that into the monitor." If you are a parent buying a first computer for a kid, you do not want to explain what a motherboard is or worry about them shorting out exposed pins. The Pi 400 is a computer you can hand to someone who has never used a computer before and they will figure it out in five minutes.

Performance: What Can It Actually Do?

Let us be honest about what the Pi 400 is and is not. It is not a gaming rig. It is not a video editing workstation. It is not going to run Adobe Premiere or render 4K video in real time. The ARM Cortex-A72 cores are capable for their class but they are still mobile-class chips running in a thermally constrained enclosure. If you try to compile Chromium from source on it, you will be waiting a while.

But here is what it CAN do, and the list is longer than most people expect:

Web browsing. Chromium runs well enough. Pages load in a second or two. JavaScript-heavy sites like YouTube and GitHub are usable. You will notice lag on particularly heavy web apps, but for reading, research, email, and social media, it is perfectly fine. The Pi 400 handles 1080p YouTube playback without issue. 4K playback is hit or miss depending on the codec.

Programming. This is where the Pi 400 shines. Python, Node.js, Go, Rust, and C all run natively on ARM Linux. The pre-installed Thonny Python IDE and Geany text editor mean you can start coding the moment you boot. The GPIO header gives you physical computing capabilities that no laptop can match. If you want to learn embedded development, robotics, or IoT, the Pi 400 is one of the best teaching platforms on the market. You can also install VS Code via apt and get a full development environment with syntax highlighting, debugging, and extensions.

Office work. LibreOffice comes pre-installed and runs smoothly. Word processing, spreadsheets, and presentations all work without noticeable lag. If your daily work involves writing documents, managing spreadsheets, and sending emails, the Pi 400 can genuinely serve as your primary machine. It will not win any benchmarks but it will get the job done.

Media consumption. It plays 1080p video smoothly. It can decode H.264 and H.265 in hardware. VLC, mpv, and Kodi all run well. For a media center connected to a living room TV, it is excellent. The dual micro HDMI outputs mean you can drive two displays, which is more than most budget laptops offer.

Retro gaming. RetroPie and Lakka both run on the Pi 400. You can emulate everything from the NES up through the PlayStation 1 and N64. Dreamcast and PSP emulation is playable with some frames dropped. Anything beyond that gets into territory where the ARM cores start to struggle, but for classic gaming, the Pi 400 is a fantastic machine.

Why This Matters Beyond the Specs

The tech industry has spent the last fifteen years pushing consumers toward increasingly expensive, increasingly closed systems. A MacBook Pro starts at $1,299. A mid-range Windows laptop is $600 to $800. Chromebooks occupy the budget tier but they are cloud-dependent and locked to Google's ecosystem. Meanwhile, the cost of a full desktop computer has crept upward while the repairability has crept downward. Soldered RAM. Soldered storage. Proprietary screws. Components glued in place. The message has been clear: you do not own your computer, you rent it from us.

The Raspberry Pi 400 pushes back against all of that. It costs $70 for the kit. Every component is replaceable. The microSD card is your storage, so you can swap it out, back it up, or run different operating systems by swapping cards. The operating system is open source. The hardware schematics are available. The GPIO header invites you to open it up and wire things to it rather than treating it as a sealed black box. If something breaks, you can diagnose it. If you want to understand how it works, you can.

This philosophy matters because it shapes who gets to participate in computing. A kid who learns on a Pi 400 understands that a computer is a collection of components running software that someone wrote. A kid who learns on an iPad understands that a computer is a magic glass slab that does things when you touch it. Both are valid experiences, but only one of them produces engineers.

The Educational Case

The Raspberry Pi Foundation has always had an educational mission, and the Pi 400 is the most education-ready product they have ever shipped. The kit includes a beginner's guide that walks through setting up the computer, using the desktop, browsing the web, and writing your first programs. The pre-installed software suite includes Scratch for visual programming, Python for text-based programming, Minecraft Pi Edition for gamified learning, and a suite of tools for working with the GPIO header.

Schools that cannot afford a lab full of MacBooks can afford a lab full of Pi 400s. A classroom set of twenty units runs about $1,400 plus the cost of displays, which many schools already have. That is less than the cost of two MacBook Airs. For a school in a low-income district, a developing country, or a community program, the Pi 400 is not a compromise. It is a lifeline.

Beyond schools, the Pi 400 is ideal for libraries, makerspaces, community centers, and after-school programs. It is cheap enough that if one gets damaged or stolen, it is not a catastrophe. It is simple enough that a volunteer with no IT background can set it up. And because it runs standard Linux, the skills you learn on it transfer directly to the servers running most of the internet.

Limitations and Honest Critiques

The Pi 400 is not perfect. The keyboard is membrane-based and feels like a laptop keyboard from 2012. It is fine for typing but mechanical keyboard enthusiasts will want to plug in an external keyboard through USB, which defeats the form factor. The integrated layout means you cannot upgrade the RAM or swap the board for a newer model without buying a whole new unit. The thermals are acceptable but sustained workloads like compilation or video encoding will throttle the CPU after a while.

Storage is limited to microSD cards, which are slower than SSDs and have lower endurance. You can connect a USB SSD for better performance, but that adds another cable and another cost. The lack of an NVMe slot is a real limitation for anyone doing disk-intensive work.

The web browsing experience, while functional, is not fast. Heavy sites with lots of JavaScript will feel sluggish. If your workflow involves Google Docs, Slack, Notion, and a dozen browser tabs, you will notice the difference between the Pi 400 and a real desktop. It is usable, but it is not snappy.

Audio output is through HDMI or the 3.5mm jack, and there is no built-in speaker or microphone. If you want to do video calls, you will need a USB webcam with a microphone. The Pi 400 does not have the processing headroom for real-time AI background blur or noise cancellation, so your Zoom experience will be basic.

Who Is It For?

The Pi 400 is for several audiences, and it is worth being precise about who benefits most:

Kids and students getting their first computer. It is safe, cheap, educational, and hard to break in any meaningful way.

Tinkerers and hobbyists who want a low-cost Linux box for projects, home automation servers, ad blockers, or learning embedded development.

Schools and educators who need affordable, repairable classroom computers that teach real computing concepts.

Writers and minimalists who want a distraction-free writing machine that cannot run the apps that eat their attention.

Anyone who needs a second computer for basic tasks and does not want to spend $500 on a Chromebook.

If you need to run Windows applications, play modern games, edit video, or do heavy development work, the Pi 400 is not for you. It is a Linux ARM machine with 4GB of RAM. Know what you are buying.

The Bigger Picture

The Raspberry Pi 400 represents something that was almost lost in the rush toward sealed, soldered, locked-down computing: the idea that a computer should be understandable, affordable, and yours. For $70, you get a machine that boots in seconds, runs free software, connects to the internet, lets you write code, and invites you to open it up and see what is inside. That is not a nostalgic throwback. That is a design philosophy that the rest of the industry should be paying attention to.

We have reached a point where the cheapest competent computer is also one of the most educational and one of the most open. That is worth celebrating. The Pi 400 will not replace your workstation, but it might replace your assumptions about what a computer has to cost, what it has to look like, and who gets to have one.
