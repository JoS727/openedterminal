When Surveillance Works Against Freedom

License-plate tracking, institutional access, and the limits public safety must respect

By Joseph Calitoy | OpenedTerminal

The question we should have asked before installing the cameras

A camera does not need to malfunction to become dangerous.

It can correctly identify the vehicle. The database can return the right location. The user can log in with a valid account. Every technical component can work, while the person searching has no legitimate reason to know where someone went.

That is the problem I want examined.

Public officials regularly ask us to judge surveillance by what it can help police accomplish. We should also judge it by the authority it creates, the people who can exercise that authority, and what happens when the justification fails.

The recent San Diego reporting makes this immediate. Voice of San Diego reported that police searched Flock records for an officer and a domestic violence survivor during a misconduct investigation. SDPD defended the searches; a city privacy-board leader questioned their compliance with policy. According to the reporting, patrol-car location records, rather than the Flock search, supplied the evidence investigators sought. That is a disputed local use, not an established court finding of illegality. It still deserves a public explanation. Voice of San Diego

The wider record shows why that explanation matters.

What this paper examines

Automated license-plate readers, or ALPRs, turn passing vehicles into searchable observations: an identifier, a location, and a time. A collection of observations can reveal patterns that no single roadside photograph establishes.

This paper primarily examines ALPR collection, retention, access, and sharing. It also considers broader surveillance systems when they illuminate the same governance problems.

Those technologies must remain distinct. A plate reader is not automatically a facial-recognition system. A police camera network is not identical to an intelligence platform. None of the international examples below establishes that Flock supplied those systems.

This is a documentary research review and policy argument. It is not a population-wide measurement of abuse, an independent product penetration test, or a claim to have interviewed the affected people.

United States: the safeguards have failed in different ways

Collection can grow faster than accountability

California’s State Auditor examined four agencies in 2020 and found that none had an ALPR policy satisfying all legally required elements. Three shared images widely without demonstrating that recipients consistently had a right and need to receive them. The audit also identified inadequate monitoring.

At LAPD, 99.9 percent of 320 million stored images concerned vehicles not on a hot list when photographed. That does not mean 99.9 percent were worthless or that every driver was necessarily uninvolved in crime. The auditor acknowledged that historical images can aid later investigations. It does establish the scale of collecting first and deciding relevance later. These are historical audit findings, not a claim that every deficiency remains unchanged today. California State Auditor

The question is proportionality. How much of everyone’s movement should be retained to support investigations involving some people?

An account can outlive the role that justified it

In July 2026, the Georgia Bureau of Investigation announced charges against a former sheriff’s-office employee who retained access to that office’s Flock account while working elsewhere. The GBI said preliminary information indicated more than 60 accesses for non-law-enforcement purposes between June 15 and July 5. The charges included stalking and misuse of a license-plate-reader system.

These are allegations and charges, not a conviction established by this review. The official account nevertheless identifies a concrete control question: why was access retained after the original employment relationship ended? Georgia Bureau of Investigation

A policy against misuse cannot substitute for revoking access that no longer belongs there.

A search can cross borders faster than protections do

In October 2025, EFF published records concerning a Texas investigation involving a self-managed abortion. EFF reported that investigators searched Flock networks spanning more than 83,000 cameras. Flock and the sheriff had described the matter as a missing-person or welfare search. The records EFF obtained instead documented a death investigation and consultation with prosecutors about possible charges. The prosecutor’s office advised that the woman could not be charged under the identified circumstances.

The distinction matters: no charge against her is established here, and a nationwide search does not prove the cameras successfully located her. But the records show why an agency’s initial public description should not end scrutiny of its purpose. EFF’s records-based investigation

Closer to home, California’s attorney general sued El Cajon in October 2025 over allegedly unlawful out-of-state ALPR sharing and pursued a further motion in January 2026. The state described sharing with more than 100 out-of-state agencies. Those are the attorney general’s allegations and enforcement position; this review did not establish a final judgment. California Department of Justice

Local collection becomes a much larger decision when someone elsewhere can search it under a different legal regime.

Public movement does not settle the constitutional question

In Leaders of a Beautiful Struggle v. Baltimore Police Department, the Fourth Circuit held in June 2021 that accessing the city’s wide-area aerial-surveillance data constituted a search and that the program’s warrantless operation violated the Fourth Amendment. The court emphasized the ability to draw conclusions from the whole of people’s movements.

That case concerned aerial surveillance, not a blanket ruling against ALPRs. It demonstrates why “you were in public” does not automatically resolve the legal implications of comprehensive, retrospective tracking. Fourth Circuit opinion

International experience: the limits are already being tested

Germany: even a non-match can implicate rights

Germany’s Federal Constitutional Court found portions of Bavaria’s automatic number-plate-recognition rules unconstitutional in a December 2018 decision, announced in February 2019. Its reasoning treated recording and comparing a person’s plate as interference with fundamental rights even when no match resulted.

The court did not prohibit every use of number-plate recognition. It required a constitutionally adequate basis and proportional limits. This is a German constitutional holding, not law governing California. Its relevance is the principle: people need not become suspects before collection affects their rights. Federal Constitutional Court

Iran: vehicle identification can enforce control over women

The UN’s 2025 fact-finding report described the Nazer application, through which vetted individuals and police could report alleged mandatory-hijab violations involving vehicles. Reports could include location, time, and plate information, producing flags and notifications. The mission also documented continuing vehicle confiscations and a wider use of surveillance to enforce restrictions on women and girls.

The mission used a “reasonable grounds to believe” standard, not the standard of a criminal conviction. Its findings describe state-backed enforcement, not simply unauthorized use by individual employees. UN Human Rights Council report

Amnesty International separately documented related practices through testimony and official records in 2024, including plate reporting and threatening messages preceding vehicle impoundment. Amnesty International

This exposes an important limit of compliance language. A system can follow the government’s instructions while helping violate fundamental freedoms.

Xinjiang: identification can feed a larger machinery of repression

Human Rights Watch’s 2019 investigation reverse-engineered a police application connected to Xinjiang’s Integrated Joint Operations Platform. It described a system combining personal information and behavioral reporting to flag people for investigation, within a broader campaign of repression against Uyghurs and other Turkic Muslims. Vehicle identifiers were among the information involved.

This was a broader data-integration and policing system, not merely a roadside plate network, and the report is historical. Its significance is how ordinary observations become more intrusive when linked to identity, relationships, and judgments about supposedly suspicious behavior. Human Rights Watch

The comparison does not make San Diego equivalent to Xinjiang or Iran. It shows why the purpose of surveillance must remain open to challenge, even when its operators are authorized.

What the vendor says has changed

Flock’s current California safeguards statement acknowledges that some California networks were inadvertently accessible to out-of-state agencies during 2025. It also says earlier logging limitations sometimes prevent determining the specific cause.

The company describes stronger controls: blocked out-of-state and federal sharing for California agencies, restrictions on immigration- and reproductive-care-related searches, standardized offense entries, and permanent logging of sharing-setting changes. These are material claims and should not be omitted. This review has not independently tested their operation or effectiveness. Flock’s California safeguards statement

If those protections work, demonstrate it. Publish independent assessments, explain exceptions, and show what happens when an agency or employee attempts a prohibited search.

The standard I propose

The failures above are not interchangeable. They call for controls at different stages.

1. Establish authority before revealing results. Every search should be tied to an actual authorized purpose, with a case reference or documented emergency justification. A text box that accepts any explanation is insufficient.

2. Limit access to the task and the person’s current role. Access should expire, require periodic reapproval, and be revoked immediately when employment or assignment changes. Shared accounts should not be permitted.

3. Treat historical movement searches as especially intrusive. Require stronger independent authorization for searches that reconstruct someone’s life over time. Legal requirements vary; this is a proposed standard, not a claim that an identical warrant rule currently applies everywhere.

4. Prevent prohibited sharing in the system itself. Restrictions should apply to the receiving agency, stated purpose, exports, and onward sharing. Do not rely solely on an administrator understanding every setting.

5. Retain less. Require a public justification for retention periods. Preserve relevant evidence under documented authority, rather than keeping everyone’s history indefinitely because storage is available.

6. Audit independently and test the controls. Reviewers should examine actual searches, access changes, emergency exceptions, and exports. Test whether prohibited actions are blocked. An audit log proves little if nobody examines it.

7. Provide a safe route to challenge misuse. People should have an independent complaint and remedy process. Transparency must not expose victims’ movements or turn public records into another stalking resource.

8. Measure public benefit honestly. Report useful investigative leads separately from arrests, convictions, recovered property, and unsupported vendor attribution. Include mistaken matches, unauthorized searches, complaints, and retention breaches.

These are proposed requirements. Their combined effectiveness has not been established by a controlled study.

The evidence still missing

A catalogue of incidents cannot tell us the overall abuse rate without knowing how many searches occurred, how reliably misuse was detected, and which incidents were excluded.

Likewise, a list of successful cases does not establish how much crime a system prevented or whether less intrusive methods could have achieved the same result.

A credible evaluation should disclose searches and unique users by purpose; unauthorized searches and detection methods; access-revocation times; independent audit coverage; cross-agency sharing and blocked requests; false matches and enforcement actions; investigative outcomes and attribution limits; and complaints, remedies, and impacts on protected activity.

Where safety requires withholding operational details, independent reviewers should still be able to inspect them.

Freedom needs an enforceable boundary

I want police to find missing people and investigate violence. Those goals deserve capable tools.

They also deserve systems that cannot quietly convert access granted for public service into access used for personal interest, political control, or unrestricted tracking.

The public should not have to wait for a survivor, a journalist, or a whistleblower to discover that the boundaries existed only in a presentation.

Before expanding surveillance, prove that its limits operate in practice. If the government or vendor cannot show that, the answer should be to restrict the system until it can.
